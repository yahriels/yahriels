10 State your hypotheses and project Aim(s). 
Hyperactive stretch-reflex circuitry, driven by the loss of descending inhibition and maladaptive spinal 
plasticity, is a principal biomechanical bottleneck that prevents many individuals with motor-incomplete 
spinal cord injury (SCI) from regaining an efficient, pain-free gait1–3. Pharmacologic options such as 
baclofen, tizanidine, and intramuscular botulinum toxin dampen reflex excitability but also weaken 
residual voluntary drive, limiting functional gains4. Our collaborative team at the National Center for 
Adaptive Neurotechnologies (NCAN) has developed a targeted plasticity approach that involves 
operant down-conditioning of the soleus H-reflex as a learning-based alternative treatment5–7. By 
progressively rewarding trials in which the reflex is smaller than a criterion, patients reduce reflex 
excitability, reduce ankle stiffness, and walk faster—even years after injury7. However, the reliance of 
H-reflex operant conditioning (HROC) on explicit performance-based rewards using real-time visual 
feedback demands continuous attention. This can be cognitively demanding for individuals particularly 
for the therapy durations required (1-hour sessions, 3x week for 12 weeks), thus limiting compliance 
and limiting participation to cognitively intact individuals. Importantly, the therapy is currently tethered 
to research laboratories thus limiting home-based therapy.  
Our team at the University of Texas at Dallas (UTD) has pioneered the use of pairing vagus nerve 
stimulation (VNS) with rehabilitation to enhance plasticity and recovery after various forms of 
neurological injury, including SCI. VNS provides precisely timed engagement of neuromodulatory 
systems8,9 that in turn drive rapid, phasic release of pro-plasticity molecules which are known to govern 
synaptic plasticity throughout brain and spinal networks10. Pairing the VNS-dependent release of 
neuromodulators with the repeated activation of CNS networks drives robust plasticity in these 
networks9,11,12. Importantly, our recent data shows that VNS can facilitate synaptic plasticity with 
minimal conscious awareness13,14. We therefore posit that substituting HROC’s visual rewards with a 
VNS-based teaching signal could convert H-reflex conditioning from a cognitively taxing, clinic-bound 
protocol into a fully automated intervention suitable for home use. Embedding the reinforcement signal 
in an implanted device retains the mechanistic specificity of operant conditioning while eliminating the 
need for prolonged concentration. 
Aim 1 will establish the safety and feasibility of VNS-reinforced HROC (V-HROC) in six adults with 
chronic (≥ 12 mo) AIS C/D spinal cord injury and gait impairments. All candidates are drawn from our 
existing pool of participants who were previously implanted with a VNS system during an early 
feasibility study15. Therefore, no additional surgery is required, greatly reducing risk and start-up time. 
Success requires at least 80% session completion and no device-related serious adverse events 
during the 12-week intervention. Aim 2 will test early efficacy by comparing pre- versus post-therapy 
soleus H-reflex amplitude, walking speed, WISCI-II, and Modified Ashworth scores. We anticipate 
significant reflex depression, faster gait, and reduced spasticity persisting four weeks after treatment, 
thereby providing preliminary evidence of VNS as a practical reward substitute. Aim 3 will elucidate the 
mechanisms of recovery by using motion-capture analysis to quantify pre- versus post-therapy 
changes in gait kinematics and overall movement quality. 
11 Provide an overview of the experimental design/methods. 
The proposed study is a prospective, single-arm pilot trial that will recruit six adults (18-65 yr) with 
chronic (≥ 12 mo) motor-incomplete spinal cord injury (AIS C/D) who present with clinically significant 
gait impairments.  Initially, we will secure regulatory approval to conduct the pilot study by amending 
our existing IRB and IDE protocols to incorporate the combination of HROC with VNS. Given the low
risk profile of HROC, we anticipate an expedited approval process, and our team has experience 
amending our active IDE for VNS in SCI to incorporate other therapy modalities. After informed 
consent, each participant completes two baseline visits during which we capture reference measures 
of reflex excitability (soleus H- and M-wave recruitment curves), spasticity (Modified Ashworth Scale), 
and functional mobility (10-m and 6-min Walk Tests, Walking Index for Spinal Cord Injury-II, and 
marker-based motion-capture gait metrics). 
The intervention spans twelve weeks and consists of three conditioning sessions per week, each 
lasting approximately one hour. Throughout the trial the participant stands while surface EMG from the 
soleus muscle is processed in real time. When background EMG is held within a low target window for

two seconds, the tibial nerve is stimulated to evoke an H-reflex. On trials in which the reflex amplitude 
falls below a session-specific criterion (chosen so that ~50 % of trials meet the target), the implant 
immediately delivers a 0.5-s train of VNS (0.8 mA, 30 Hz, 100 µs pulse width; parameters known to 
facilitate neuroplasticity). No visual or auditory feedback of performance is provided. The burst of VNS 
serves as the only reinforcement signal, thereby testing the central hypothesis that neuromodulator 
release can replace conscious reward cues and enable attention-free, potentially home-based 
conditioning. Each visit comprises 240 reflex trials, yielding roughly 120 contingent VNS deliveries— a 
dosing paradigm matched to our prior work in both HROC and VNS. 
To control for non-associative effects, the first two weeks employ a “control” version of the protocol in 
which reflexes are recorded but no reinforcement (VNS) is delivered; these data provide a within
subject baseline. Functional outcomes are repeated at week 6 (mid-therapy), week 12 (end of therapy), 
and four weeks after therapy to examine durability. Safety is monitored continuously; adverse events 
are reviewed by an independent physician. Feasibility is defined as completion of the target 120 VNS 
deliveries in at least 80% of scheduled sessions. 
Primary efficacy endpoints are the percent change in soleus H-reflex amplitude and walking speed. 
Secondary endpoints include WISCI-II score, Modified Ashworth grade, cadence, stride length, and 
step-length asymmetry. Exploratory motion-capture analyses will examine stance-swing ratio, double
support time, step-width variability, and key joint angles (hip extension, swing-phase knee flexion, 
push-off ankle plantar-flexion) to clarify balance and propulsion changes. Outcomes will be analyzed 
with linear mixed-effects models that treat time as a fixed factor and participant as a random factor. 
Video recordings of all clinical assessments will be scored by blinded raters to minimize bias. Data 
analysis will be performed in Python or MATLAB, and de-identified datasets will be released on 
OpenNeuro six months after study completion, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. This 
pragmatic design rigorously evaluates whether contingent VNS can act as an intrinsic reward signal to 
drive reflex down-conditioning and improve gait while laying the groundwork for a future, randomized 
controlled trial. 
12 Explain the project’s relevance to the Neilsen Foundation. 
The proposed study addresses one of the largest barriers to mobility after incomplete SCI—
hyperactive stretch reflexes that generate inefficient gait—with a therapy that is mechanistically 
grounded and immediately translatable. By substituting a precisely timed burst of VNS for the visual 
reward normally used in HROC, the project leverages an existing FDA-approved neuromodulatory 
therapy16 to create an intrinsic, attention-free reinforcement signal. Eliminating the need for conscious 
engagement removes a major obstacle that has so far limited HROC to highly supervised laboratory 
settings. It opens the door to home-based, scalable rehabilitation that can reach rural and underserved 
populations the Neilsen Foundation is committed to supporting. 
The work sits well within the SCIRTS Pilot Clinical category. It builds on decades of compelling pre
clinical and randomized controlled clinical trials, rigorously evaluates safety and feasibility in a well
defined cohort, and generates the preliminary efficacy evidence required for a subsequent randomized 
trial.  Because all participants are already implanted with the ReStore VNS device from our previous 
study15, and our team will amend our existing IDE, the project is cost-efficient and low-risk, allowing 
Neilsen resources to be leveraged for high-impact questions rather than surgical and regulatory costs. 
The protocol’s reliance on objective electrophysiological and functional outcomes, its preregistered 
open-data plan, and its close integration with a multidisciplinary rehabilitation team at UT Dallas and 
Baylor Research Institute embody the Foundation’s emphasis on rigor, transparency, and clinical 
relevance. If contingent VNS can indeed replace external rewards while amplifying therapeutic benefit, 
the findings will redefine how HROC is delivered. It will provide the SCI community with a practical, 
non-pharmacological option to regain safer, faster walking and greater autonomy. It will also justify 
exploring the use of VNS-reinforced HROC (V-HROC) in the upper limb. These goals align precisely 
with the Neilsen Foundation’s mission to change lives after spinal cord injury.
