That captures both the mechanism ("why") and the experimental prediction without overclaiming superiority.

Abstract Writing Basic Protocol and Pointers
Write the first draft of your abstract, which should include all of the above-mentioned materials that specifically and adequately reflect the experiments included within the study
-	The first sentence should set the scene of the place where there is a scientific unknown (or major problem).
-	By the second sentence the protagonist should be introduced, along with his major problem.
-	Antagonist/s should also be introduced no later than the second or third sentence. It is essential to have the antagonists in direct conflict (scientific unknown) with the protagonist.
-	Next should be 2 (maximum 3) sentences describing the results of experiments centered around the protagonist that answer aspects of the major problem, which are an increase in the Tension (T1, T2, and maybe T3). Tension in your scientific story is increased incrementally by experimentation. One experiment should lead to a logical mini-resolution, then the following experiment should build upon the previous one. The increase in tension also provides character development of the protagonist. The increase in tensions (T1-T3) brings the Scientific Story to the point of the climax experiment
-	The climax experiment should be written in one sentence clearly  describing a follow-up experiment that validates the climax experiment to relax the tension that was built throughout the beginning and middle of the story before the climax
-	The last one to two sentences should bring the protagonist back to the original scene with the original problem resolved.
-	Note: Given the space and word count limitations for Abstracts, some of these sentences can be combined. 


PART I — CONFLICT SETUP
Sentence 1 — Scene / Scientific Unknown
Main idea: Hyperreflexia after neurological injury is clinically significant and current treatments have a critical unresolved limitation.

Hyperactive stretch-reflex circuitry, driven by loss of descending inhibition and maladaptive spinal plasticity, is a major contributor to spasticity after neurological injury
This creates a principal biomechanical bottleneck: impairs efficient, pain-free gait and functional movement in neurologically injured individuals
Scientific unknown to state: Can spinal reflex excitability be selectively reduced using a learning-based approach that does not require sustained cognitive engagement or clinic-based administration?
What NOT to include here: Your protagonist (HROC), your methods, or VNS — the scene sets the problem only
Sentence 2 — Protagonist Introduced
Main idea: HROC is a learning-based targeted plasticity approach capable of reducing reflex excitability, but it comes with a structural limitation that creates the conflict.

40+ years of pre-clinical research has established operant conditioning of the soleus H-reflex as a mechanism for reducing spinal reflex excitability
HROC progressively rewards trials in which H-reflex amplitude falls below a criterion threshold, driving adaptive spinal plasticity
Pre-clinical data: operant conditioning can up-condition or down-condition the H-reflex to modulate reflex-dependent behavior (qualify — animal data shows reflex modulation, not hyperreflexia treatment)
Early-stage clinical data (qualify as investigational only — MEC2): preliminary results suggest patients reduce reflex excitability and improve functional outcomes
The protagonist's flaw: HROC depends on explicit, attention-demanding reinforcement (food reward / real-time visual feedback) delivered over prolonged training schedules
Sentence 3 — Antagonist(s) in Direct Conflict
Main idea: The dependency on explicit reinforcement is the direct antagonist — it blocks HROC from becoming a scalable, accessible therapy.

Antagonist 1 — Cognitive demand: HROC relies on explicit performance-based rewards requiring real-time, sustained participant attention — cognitively taxing over 1-hour sessions, 3× per week for months
Antagonist 2 — Training duration: 50 days pre-clinically; ~3 months clinically — prolonged timelines reduce compliance and translational feasibility
Antagonist 3 — Access limitation: Currently tethered to research laboratories — cannot be deployed for home-based therapy
Antagonist 4 — Population limitation: Limits participation to cognitively intact individuals
The conflict: These antagonists together block HROC from reaching patients who need it most
Merge note: S2 + S3 should be combined. Combined content builds from "HROC works but..." — hero with a fatal flaw.
