Spacing, Not Just Quantity: Dosing Rules for Vagus Nerve Stimulation–Paired Tactile Rehabilitation (Ruiz et al. 2023)

Introduction
Chronic somatosensory loss is a frequent consequence of peripheral nerve injury and stroke, and conventional sensory retraining yields only modest benefit (Darrow et al. 2020). Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) paired with rehabilitation, termed targeted plasticity therapy, delivers brief bursts of stimulation that engage cholinergic and noradrenergic neuromodulatory systems at the moment a sensory or motor event occurs, thereby reinforcing the circuits active during training. Ruiz et al. (2023) asked how that stimulation must be scheduled to restore somatosensation: how many pairings per session, how many sessions per week, and over how many weeks.

Before
Darrow et al. (2020) established efficacy. In rats with chronic forepaw sensory loss after median and ulnar nerve transection, VNS paired with tactile rehabilitation produced a significant, nearly complete recovery of mechanosensory withdrawal thresholds that persisted for months, whereas equivalent rehabilitation without VNS produced no improvement. Only a single intensive schedule was tested, however, so the minimum effective dose was unknown.

Hays et al. (2014) showed, in a rat model of ischemic stroke, that both timing and amount constrain efficacy. VNS delivered 2 hours after training, or several-fold more VNS delivered during training, each yielded significantly less recovery of forelimb strength than VNS paired with successful movements. More stimulation was therefore not automatically better.

Borland et al. (2018) varied the inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of VNS–tone pairing in auditory cortex. A shortened ISI still drove significant cortical map plasticity in naive rats, but it failed to normalize neural and behavioral responses after acoustic trauma, and reducing the number of pairings abolished plasticity altogether.

Excess stimulation was thus known to be harmful, but whether a fixed number of paired events could be redistributed, whether concentrated into fewer or faster sessions, without loss of benefit had not been tested. Ruiz et al. (2023) report that they initially expected total pairing number to be the primary determinant of recovery. The question mattered clinically: the validated regimen demanded frequent sessions over many weeks, a substantial barrier to adherence, so a compressed schedule would ease translation.

The Paper
Eight weeks after median and ulnar nerve transection, female rats received tactile rehabilitation paired with 0.5-second VNS bursts under one of six schedules, compared with rehabilitation alone (n = 15 per group). Schedules varied pairings per session (50, 200, or 800), sessions per week (1 or 4), duration (1 or 4 weeks), and ISI (2 or 10 seconds) (Ruiz et al. 2023).

The decisive result is the comparison among groups matched for total dose (3,200 pairings). Only Moderate Daily VNS (200 pairings per session, 4 sessions per week, 4 weeks, 10-second ISI) significantly reduced withdrawal thresholds relative to rehabilitation without VNS. Two comparisons isolate single variables. Intense Weekly VNS (800 pairings once weekly for 4 weeks, 10-second ISI) differed only in distribution and failed to enhance recovery. Rapid Daily VNS (identical to Moderate Daily except for a 2-second ISI) differed only in spacing and also failed. Rapid Intense Daily VNS (800 pairings per session over a single week at a 2-second ISI) failed as well, although it altered several parameters simultaneously and cannot be attributed to any one of them. Reducing total dose to 800 pairings (Short Daily, Moderate Weekly) likewise failed (Ruiz et al. 2023).

This result complicated, rather than cleanly refuted, prior thinking. Total pairing number is necessary but not sufficient; per-session load and spacing independently constrain efficacy. The authors attribute this to desensitization of neuromodulatory systems, which would impose a ceiling on effective pairings per session, but they did not measure it directly. Moreover, with 15 animals per group, a failure to reach significance does not exclude smaller benefits. The ISI finding reproduces the pattern of Borland et al. (2018) in a second sensory system: short spacing that may suffice for plasticity in intact cortex fails to support recovery after injury.

After
Malley et al. (2024) used a schedule based on the Moderate Daily regimen (200 pairings per session, 4 days per week) to probe mechanism. Moderate-intensity VNS outperformed both lower and higher intensities, an inverted-U relationship mirroring the dosing ceiling. VNS increased cortical Arc transcription in proportion to recovery, and cortical acetylcholine depletion abolished the benefit. This supports and extends Ruiz et al. by localizing the effect to cholinergic cortical plasticity, although it does not test desensitization itself.

Kilgard et al. (2025) carried the principle into humans. In a randomized, sham-controlled trial in 19 people with chronic incomplete cervical spinal cord injury, closed-loop VNS delivered approximately 340 pairings per session across 36 sessions and improved arm and hand strength and daily function. The authors cite the requirement of hundreds of pairings per day over many weeks as a design rationale. The trial supports the general "high dose, many weeks" principle, but it measured motor rather than sensory outcomes, used roughly three sessions per week, and did not vary dose, so it is consistent with Ruiz et al. rather than a test of their result.

Epperson et al. (2024) addressed the translational burden that Ruiz et al. flagged. They validated an algorithm that automatically triggers VNS on the strongest movements while maintaining a target interval between stimulations, reducing reliance on a therapist. This extends Ruiz et al. by making spaced, high-volume delivery feasible without supervision, although it was developed for motor rehabilitation.

Two limitations remain open. First, the desensitization hypothesis is untested: confirming it would require tracking neuromodulator release across successive pairings, which neither Ruiz et al. nor subsequent studies have done. Second, the human dose–response relationship is unknown, because clinical trials fix dose by design and dose-finding studies with implanted devices are costly and slow. Longer ISIs and male animals also remain untested.

Conclusion
Ruiz et al. (2023) showed that effective VNS therapy depends on how stimulation is distributed, not merely on how much is delivered. These results constrain clinical protocol design and motivate closed-loop systems that deliver spaced, high-volume stimulation without constant supervision.
