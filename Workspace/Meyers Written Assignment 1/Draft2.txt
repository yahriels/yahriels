This report examines "Effective Delivery of Vagus Nerve Stimulation Requires Many Stimulations Per Session and Many Sessions Per Week Over Many Weeks to Improve Recovery of Somatosensation" (Ruiz et al. 2023). Conventional retraining yields only modest benefit for chronic somatosensory loss (Darrow et al. 2020). In targeted plasticity therapy, brief vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) bursts engage cholinergic and noradrenergic systems during a training event, reinforcing the circuits active at that moment. I selected three papers Ruiz et al. cite (Darrow et al. 2020; Hays et al. 2014; Borland et al. 2018) and three that cite it (Chen et al. 2025; Nandakumar et al. 2024; Puri 2026).

Before
Prior work supported a two-part rule: VNS must be precisely paired with training, and enough paired events must be delivered. Excess unpaired stimulation was known to be counterproductive, but whether a fixed number of paired events could be redistributed into fewer, larger, or faster sessions was untested. Ruiz et al. (2023) state that they expected total pairing number to be the primary determinant of recovery.

Darrow et al. 2020 – In a preregistered study of rats with chronic forepaw sensory loss after median and ulnar nerve transection, VNS paired with tactile rehabilitation produced a significant, nearly complete recovery of mechanosensory withdrawal thresholds that persisted for months after stimulation ended and generalized to other measures of forepaw function, whereas equivalent rehabilitation without VNS produced no improvement (Darrow et al. 2020). Only one intensive schedule was tested, leaving the minimum effective dose unknown.

Hays et al. 2014 – In a rat stroke model, VNS delivered 2 hours after training, or several-fold more VNS delivered during training, each yielded significantly less recovery of forelimb strength than VNS paired with successful movements, although both still outperformed rehabilitation alone (Hays et al. 2014). Timing and amount both mattered, but this was shown only for motor recovery and excess stimulation.

Borland et al. 2018 – In auditory cortex, a shortened inter-stimulus interval (ISI) between VNS–tone pairings still drove significant map plasticity in naive rats but failed to normalize neural and behavioral responses after acoustic trauma, whereas reducing the number of pairings abolished plasticity (Borland et al. 2018). Spacing thus mattered more after injury, but its role in somatosensory recovery was untested.

The question was worth testing for two reasons. Clinically, "a high-volume, long-duration therapy dosage poses a practical challenge" for translation (Ruiz et al. 2023), so a compressed schedule would ease adoption. Scientifically, it tested whether spacing rules from motor and auditory cortex generalize to sensory recovery.

The Paper
Ruiz et al. 2023 – Eight weeks after nerve transection, female rats received VNS-paired tactile rehabilitation under one of six schedules or rehabilitation alone (n = 15 per group), varying pairings per session (50–800), sessions per week (1 or 4), duration (1 or 4 weeks), and ISI (2 or 10 seconds) (Ruiz et al. 2023). The result doing the most work is the comparison among groups matched for total dose (3,200 pairings). Only Moderate Daily VNS (200 pairings per session, 4 sessions per week, 4 weeks, 10-second ISI) significantly reduced withdrawal thresholds relative to rehabilitation alone (Bonferroni-corrected P = .01). Intense Weekly VNS (800 pairings once weekly), which changed only the distribution of pairings, failed, as did Rapid Daily VNS, which changed only the ISI to 2 seconds. Rapid Intense Daily VNS (800 pairings per session over one week, 2-second ISI) also failed, though it altered several parameters at once.

This result complicated rather than refuted prior thinking. It confirmed that more stimulation is not automatically better (Hays et al. 2014) and that short spacing fails after injury (Borland et al. 2018), but it overturned the authors' own expectation, showing that "total amount of VNS pairings alone is not the sole determinant" of recovery (Ruiz et al. 2023). The authors propose that neuromodulatory systems are "prone to desensitization," imposing a ceiling on effective pairings per session, but did not measure this. With 15 rats per group, failed schedules may carry smaller benefits.

After
Chen et al. 2025 – Chen et al.'s review of VNS for hand rehabilitation states that Ruiz et al. "examined the optimal timing of VNS intervention to tactile rehabilitation" and groups the study with others using a 100 µs pulse width (Chen et al. 2025). This supports the original result but narrows it to timing and pulse parameters, omitting the finding that distribution across sessions matters. Chen et al. also frame VNS as potentially non-invasive, although Ruiz et al. used implanted VNS.

Nandakumar et al. 2024 – Nandakumar et al. cite Ruiz et al. specifically for showing that "the interval between VNS event pairings within sessions (Borland et al., 2018) and across sessions/weeks (Ruiz et al., 2023b), influences neuroplasticity and recovery" (Nandakumar et al. 2024). They extend this by proposing that patient state is the next variable to control, timing VNS to cognitive state and cardiorespiratory rhythms. This offers a candidate route around the per-session ceiling.

Puri 2026 – Puri's short review proposes that transcutaneous auricular VNS could remodel mesocorticolimbic reward circuitry in opioid use disorder across acute, intermediate, and long-term timescales (Puri 2026). Invoking Ruiz et al.'s requirement for repeated sessions over weeks, Puri extends the principle beyond sensory rehabilitation while acknowledging that "these mechanistic links remain hypotheses" and that direct evidence "remains lacking."

All three papers support Ruiz et al.'s interpretation and none overturns it, but each extends the result by extrapolation rather than experiment: to non-invasive stimulation, to state-dependent delivery, and to non-sensory circuits. Two questions the authors flagged remain open. First, they noted that "longer intervals between pairings could produce larger effects, an avenue we did not explore" (Ruiz et al. 2023). Second, desensitization as the cause of the ceiling has not been tested, which would require measuring neuromodulator release across successive pairings. Both persist partly because follow-up work has shifted toward closed-loop timing and non-invasive devices rather than dose-finding and mechanism, and because clinical trials fix dose by design. Ruiz et al. established that the distribution of VNS, not only its quantity, determines recovery, but why remains unresolved.
