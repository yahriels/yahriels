For this assignment, I chose "Effective Delivery of Vagus Nerve Stimulation Requires Many Stimulations Per Session and Many Sessions Per Week Over Many Weeks to Improve Recovery of Somatosensation" (Ruiz et al. 2023). Chronic somatosensory loss after stroke or peripheral nerve injury is common, and conventional sensory retraining yields only modest benefit (Darrow et al. 2020). Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) paired with rehabilitation, termed targeted plasticity therapy, delivers brief stimulation bursts that engage cholinergic and noradrenergic neuromodulatory systems at the moment a training event occurs, reinforcing the circuits active at that time. VNS-paired tactile rehabilitation was known to work, but only under one demanding schedule. The open question was whether the therapeutic dose could be compressed, delivered in fewer, larger, or faster sessions, without losing efficacy; the authors state that they initially expected total pairing number to be the primary determinant of recovery (Ruiz et al. 2023). I selected three papers Ruiz et al. cite (Darrow et al. 2020; Hays et al. 2014; Borland et al. 2018) and three that cite it (Chen et al. 2025; Nandakumar et al. 2024; Puri 2026).

Before
Darrow et al. 2020 – This study established efficacy. In rats with chronic forepaw sensory loss after median and ulnar nerve transection, VNS paired with tactile rehabilitation produced significant, nearly complete recovery of mechanosensory withdrawal thresholds that persisted for months after stimulation ended, whereas equivalent rehabilitation without VNS produced no improvement (Darrow et al. 2020). Because only one intensive schedule was tested, the minimum effective dose remained unknown. Ruiz et al. considered this gap worth testing because a regimen requiring frequent sessions for many weeks is a major barrier to adherence and clinical adoption.

Hays et al. 2014 – Hays et al. showed in a rat stroke model that both timing and amount constrain efficacy. VNS delivered 2 hours after training, or several-fold more VNS delivered during training, each yielded significantly less recovery of forelimb strength than VNS paired with successful movements (Hays et al. 2014). More stimulation was not automatically better, but whether such constraints govern sensory recovery was unknown.

Borland et al. 2018 – Borland et al. varied the inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of VNS–tone pairing in auditory cortex. A shortened ISI still drove significant cortical map plasticity in naive rats, but it failed to normalize neural and behavioral responses after acoustic trauma, and reducing the number of pairings abolished plasticity (Borland et al. 2018).
The Paper
Ruiz et al. 2023 – Eight weeks after median and ulnar nerve transection, female rats received tactile rehabilitation paired with 0.5-second VNS bursts under one of six schedules or rehabilitation alone (n = 15 per group), varying pairings per session (50–800), sessions per week (1 or 4), duration (1 or 4 weeks), and ISI (2 or 10 seconds) (Ruiz et al. 2023). The result doing the most work is the comparison among groups matched for total dose (3,200 pairings). Only Moderate Daily VNS (200 pairings per session, 4 sessions per week, 4 weeks, 10-second ISI) significantly reduced withdrawal thresholds relative to rehabilitation alone. Intense Weekly VNS (800 pairings once weekly), which changed only the distribution of pairings, failed, as did Rapid Daily VNS, which changed only the ISI to 2 seconds. Rapid Intense Daily VNS (800 pairings per session over one week, 2-second ISI) also failed, though it altered several parameters at once. This result complicated rather than refuted prior thinking: total dose is necessary but not sufficient, because per-session load and spacing independently limit efficacy. The authors attribute this to desensitization of neuromodulatory systems, which would impose a ceiling on effective pairings per session, but did not measure it, and with 15 animals per group, non-significant schedules may carry smaller benefits.

After
Chen et al. 2025 – Chen et al. reviewed VNS for hand-function rehabilitation and cited Ruiz et al. as preclinical evidence that VNS enhances sensory recovery, identifying protocol optimization as a priority for future work (Chen et al. 2025). This supports the original interpretation, but it also reframes it: Chen et al. present VNS as a potentially non-invasive therapy, whereas Ruiz et al. used implanted cervical VNS, so whether their dosing rules transfer to transcutaneous stimulation is untested.

Nandakumar et al. 2024 – Nandakumar et al. cited Ruiz et al. alongside Borland et al. as evidence that the interval between pairings, within sessions and across sessions and weeks, shapes plasticity and recovery (Nandakumar et al. 2024). They extend this framework by arguing that patient state is the next variable to control, proposing closed-loop systems that time VNS to cognitive state and cardiorespiratory rhythms. Ruiz et al.'s fixed schedule thus becomes a baseline for state-dependent delivery to improve upon.

Puri 2026 – Puri's short review proposes a multi-timescale framework in which transcutaneous auricular VNS could remodel mesocorticolimbic reward circuitry in opioid use disorder, spanning acute noradrenergic effects to longer-term BDNF/TrkB-associated synaptic remodeling (Puri 2026). Citing Ruiz et al.'s requirement for repeated sessions over weeks, Puri extends the principle beyond sensory rehabilitation, while acknowledging that these mechanisms remain hypotheses and that direct evidence in opioid use disorder is lacking.

None of these papers overturns Ruiz et al., but none tests their result directly either. Instead, the interpretation has broadened: a schedule validated for implanted VNS in one rat model is now cited as a rule for non-invasive stimulation and non-sensory circuits, an extrapolation that remains untested. The limitation the authors flagged, that neuromodulatory desensitization underlies the ceiling, remains unresolved, because confirming it would require measuring neuromodulator release across successive pairings. The human dose–response relationship is likewise unknown, because clinical trials typically fix dose by design and dose-finding with implanted devices is costly and slow.

Our class discussion emphasized that more stimulation can be counterproductive, which led me to ask whether closed-loop, state-dependent delivery could bypass the ceiling Ruiz et al. identified (Nandakumar et al. 2024) and whether the principle generalizes across circuits (Puri 2026). Ruiz et al. (2023) showed that the distribution of stimulation, not only its quantity, determines recovery, a constraint that now informs both clinical protocols and closed-loop device design.
